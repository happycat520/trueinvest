Every earnings season brings a wave of headlines about insider buying and selling.

"CEO buys 10,000 shares."

"Director sells $5 million worth of stock."

Financial media often treats these transactions as if they automatically predict the stock's future.

They don't.

Most insider transactions tell us very little.

A small number, however, can be incredibly informative.

Learning to distinguish between the two has become one of the most valuable parts of my investment process.

## Why I Pay Attention to Insider Buying

Corporate insiders possess something outside investors never will:

An intimate understanding of their business.

They know whether customer conversations are improving.

They understand the competitive landscape.

They see operational trends before quarterly reports are released.

They know whether strategic initiatives are gaining traction.

Of course, they cannot legally trade on material non-public information.

But they do have a much deeper appreciation of the company's long-term prospects than outside investors.

That makes their behavior worth studying.

Notice I said **studying**, not blindly following.

## Buying Sends a Stronger Signal Than Selling

One of the biggest mistakes investors make is treating insider buying and insider selling as equally meaningful.

They are not.

Executives sell stock for countless reasons.

They may want to diversify.

They may owe taxes.

They may be buying a home.

They may simply have a pre-arranged selling program.

The sale itself often tells us nothing about the business.

Buying is different.

When executives voluntarily spend their own money to purchase shares on the open market, there are far fewer possible explanations.

In most cases, they believe the stock is worth more than the current price.

That doesn't guarantee they are right.

But it is information worth incorporating into the overall investment thesis.

## Not All Insider Buying Is Created Equal

A CEO purchasing $20,000 worth of stock sounds encouraging.

Until you discover the executive earns several million dollars a year.

The purchase may represent less than one week's compensation.

That is not necessarily a strong signal.

Instead, I ask several questions.

### Is the Purchase Meaningful?

The dollar amount matters.

A purchase that represents a significant personal investment carries more weight than a symbolic purchase designed to generate positive headlines.

Meaningful commitment often matters more than the absolute number of shares.

## Is It an Open-Market Purchase?

This distinction is critical.

Stock grants.

Restricted stock awards.

Option exercises.

Performance compensation.

These are not the same as voluntarily buying shares in the open market.

I focus almost exclusively on purchases made with the insider's own capital.

That is where incentives are most closely aligned with outside shareholders.

## Are Multiple Executives Buying?

One insider buying can be interesting.

Several independent insiders buying within a short period is much more compelling.

When directors, the CEO, and senior executives all independently decide the stock is attractive, I pay close attention.

Clusters of insider purchases often deserve additional research.

## Is the Stock Already Unpopular?

This may be the most important question.

Insider buying tends to be most valuable when sentiment is extremely negative.

If a stock is making new highs and investors are already optimistic, insider purchases add relatively little new information.

But when a company has become deeply unpopular and insiders continue buying aggressively, the signal becomes much more interesting.

Markets frequently become excessively pessimistic.

Insiders sometimes recognize that before the broader market does.

## Insider Buying Is Not an Investment Thesis

This is where many investors go wrong.

They purchase a stock simply because the CEO bought shares.

That is not investing.

That is outsourcing your analysis.

Insider activity should never replace fundamental research.

Instead, it should prompt additional questions.

Why are they buying now?

Has valuation become unusually attractive?

Has the market become too pessimistic?

Are there catalysts the market is underappreciating?

The insider purchase is the beginning of the investigation—not the conclusion.

## When Insider Buying Can Be Misleading

Even experienced executives make mistakes.

History contains plenty of examples where insiders purchased stock before prices fell much further.

Being knowledgeable about a business does not mean they can predict recessions, industry disruptions, or changes in investor sentiment.

Sometimes insiders buy too early.

Sometimes they underestimate structural challenges.

Sometimes they simply prove wrong.

That is why insider buying should increase conviction only when it aligns with a broader investment thesis built on valuation, financial strength, competitive positioning, and potential catalysts.

## How Insider Buying Fits Into My Process

For me, insider activity is one piece of a larger puzzle.

When I identify a company that appears undervalued, I naturally become more interested if executives are also purchasing shares with their own money.

The alignment is encouraging.

It suggests that management and outside shareholders are making similar judgments about value.

But if the valuation is unattractive or the business fundamentals continue deteriorating, insider buying alone is not enough.

Good investing comes from assembling multiple pieces of evidence—not relying on any single indicator.

## The Most Valuable Signal

Over time, I have found that the strongest insider signal often has three characteristics:

* The purchases are made in the open market.
* They are meaningful relative to the insider's personal wealth or compensation.
* They occur during periods of widespread pessimism.

When all three align, I believe investors should pay attention.

Not because insiders are always right.

But because the odds may be shifting in your favor.

## Final Thoughts

Investing is rarely about finding certainty.

It is about improving probabilities.

Insider buying does not guarantee future returns.

Neither does discounted valuation, attractive cash flow, or a strong balance sheet.

But when several independent pieces of evidence point in the same direction, confidence in the investment thesis naturally increases.

At CrystalWell, we don't view insider buying as a shortcut.

We view it as one of many valuable signals that can help identify situations where the market's expectations have become disconnected from reality.

Sometimes the most interesting investment opportunities emerge when the people who know the business best quietly begin buying while everyone else is looking the other way.
